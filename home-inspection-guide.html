The Home Inspection: Your Last Line of Defense

You're under contract, congratulations! But before you celebrate, there's one critical step that separates informed buyers from those who inherit expensive surprises.

Phase 5: Contract to Close Stage 5.1: Due Diligence Resource 5.1A

What You'll Learn

The average home inspection uncovers $10,000-$15,000 in issues buyers weren't aware of. This guide shows you how to use the inspection process strategically, not just to find problems, but to make informed decisions about the biggest purchase of your life.

What Is a Home Inspection?

A home inspection is a comprehensive, non-invasive visual examination of a home's major systems and components. It's performed by a licensed professional who will spend 2-4 hours crawling through attics, testing outlets, running water, and documenting everything they find.

Think of it like taking a used car to a mechanic before buying, except the stakes are hundreds of thousands of dollars higher, and you can't just trade it in if things go wrong.

✓ What It IS
  • Visual examination of accessible areas
  • Assessment of current condition
  • Identification of safety concerns
  • Educational opportunity for you
  • Documentation for future reference
✗ What It ISN'T
  • A guarantee that nothing will break
  • A pass/fail test for the home
  • A code compliance inspection
  • A tool to renegotiate the entire deal
  • Destructive testing (no walls opened)
Industry Truth

The inspection isn't about finding a "perfect" home, it's about knowing exactly what you're buying. Every home has issues. The question is whether they're deal-breakers, negotiating chips, or just things to budget for later.

What Home Inspectors Actually Examine

A thorough home inspection covers the home's major systems from roof to foundation. Here's what your inspector will evaluate:

Roof & Exterior

The first line of defense against Texas weather.

  • Shingle/roofing material condition
  • Flashing around vents and chimneys
  • Gutters and downspouts
  • Exterior siding/brick condition

Foundation & Structure

Critical in DFW's expansive clay soil region.

  • Foundation cracks (cosmetic vs. structural)
  • Evidence of movement or settling
  • Floor levelness and bounce
  • Wall cracks and door alignment

Electrical System

Safety and capacity for modern living.

  • Main panel condition and capacity
  • Wiring type and condition
  • GFCI protection in wet areas
  • Smoke and CO detector placement

Plumbing

Water in, water out, seems simple until it isn't.

  • Water heater age and condition
  • Supply line material and condition
  • Water pressure and flow
  • Signs of leaks or water damage

HVAC System

In Texas, this isn't optional, it's survival.

  • System age and expected lifespan
  • Heating and cooling function test
  • Ductwork condition and insulation
  • Filter and maintenance evidence

Interior & Finishes

Beyond cosmetics, what's hiding underneath.

  • Window and door operation
  • Wall and ceiling condition
  • Floor condition and stability
  • Signs of water intrusion

Red Flags: Understanding Severity Levels

Not all inspection findings are created equal. Here's how to categorize what you see in your report:

Critical / Deal Breaker

Walk away or demand major concession. Expensive, dangerous, or both. Cost: $10,000+

  • Major foundation failure
  • Extensive mold or water damage
  • Significant structural damage
  • Faulty electrical panel (Federal Pacific)
  • Active termite infestation with damage

Serious / Negotiate

Request repairs or credits. Significant but not deal-breakers. Cost: $2,000-$10,000

  • HVAC system near end of life
  • Roof with 2-3 years remaining
  • Outdated electrical needing updates
  • Water heater past expected lifespan
  • Evidence of past foundation repair

Moderate / Budget For

Note for future planning. Needs attention within 1-5 years, not urgent.

  • HVAC system 10-15 years old
  • Minor foundation cracks (cosmetic)
  • Older but functional water heater
  • Some window seal failures
  • Minor grading issues

Minor / Maintenance

Normal homeowner items. Every home has these, not negotiation items.

  • Missing caulk around fixtures
  • GFCI outlets to install
  • Weatherstripping on doors
  • Smoke detector batteries
  • Loose doorknobs or hinges

DFW-Specific Inspection Concerns

North Texas homes face unique challenges. Make sure your inspection addresses these regional issues:

Foundation Issues (The Big One)

DFW sits on expansive clay soil that swells when wet and shrinks when dry. This constant movement causes more foundation problems here than almost anywhere in the country. Look for: sticking doors, cracks in walls (especially above doors/windows), uneven floors, gaps at baseboards. If in doubt, get a structural engineer's evaluation, it's worth the $300-$500.

Storm Damage History

DFW gets hit by hail regularly. Ask about the roof's age and whether it's been replaced after storm damage. Check for: patched areas, mismatched shingles, and get a roof certification if the home is older than 10 years or has had recent claims.

Older Home Concerns

Homes built before 1980 may have: cast iron drain pipes that corrode internally, galvanized supply lines that restrict flow, older electrical panels (Federal Pacific and Zinsco are red flags), and original HVAC systems way past their lifespan.

New Construction Issues

Don't skip inspections on new builds! Common issues include: rushed finish work, HVAC problems from construction debris, grading issues, and punch list items the builder "forgot." The builder's warranty doesn't cover everything.

How to Use Your Inspection Report

The inspection report is a powerful tool, but only if you use it strategically. Here are your options:

Option 1: Request Repairs

Ask the seller to fix specific items before closing. Works best for safety issues, code violations, and items that require licensed contractors. Be specific about what you want repaired.

Option 2: Request Credit

Ask for a credit at closing to handle repairs yourself. Often preferred because you control the quality of work. Get real quotes to justify your request.

Option 3: Price Reduction

Negotiate a lower purchase price based on needed repairs. This reduces your loan amount and potentially your down payment. Best for significant issues.

Option 4: Accept As-Is

Proceed with the purchase knowing about the issues. Sometimes this is the right call, if the price already reflects the home's condition or if issues are minor.

Option 5: Walk Away

Terminate during the option period and get your earnest money back (minus the option fee). The right choice if you discover deal-breaker issues.

Option 6: Combination

Request repairs for some items, credits for others, and accept minor issues as-is. Most negotiations end up here, a balanced approach that keeps the deal moving.

Smart Negotiation Tips

  • Focus on Big Items: Don't nickel-and-dime the seller over $50 repairs. Focus your negotiation energy on items that cost $500+ or pose safety concerns.
  • Get Real Quotes: A repair request backed by actual contractor quotes is much more compelling than "we want $5,000 for the HVAC."
  • Know Market Conditions: In a seller's market, you have less leverage. In a buyer's market, sellers are more willing to negotiate.
  • Be Reasonable: Every home has issues. Asking for too much can kill a deal. Focus on legitimate concerns, not a wish list of upgrades.

How to Choose a Home Inspector

Not all home inspectors are created equal. Here's what to look for:

Must-Haves

  • TREC License: In Texas, home inspectors must be licensed by the Texas Real Estate Commission (TREC). Verify their license status online.
  • Insurance: Errors & omissions (E&O) insurance protects you if they miss something significant.
  • Experience: Look for inspectors with 500+ inspections or 3+ years of experience, especially with DFW homes.
  • Sample Report: Ask to see a sample report before hiring. It should be detailed, include photos, and be easy to understand.
  • Availability for Questions: A good inspector welcomes you at the inspection and answers questions afterward.

Red Flags to Avoid

  • Inspectors who don't let you attend the inspection
  • Reports delivered days later instead of within 24 hours
  • Inspectors who also offer to do repairs (conflict of interest)
  • Rock-bottom prices that seem too good to be true
  • No physical access to the inspector (can't call with questions)

Key Takeaways

  • Schedule your inspection within 24-48 hours of going under contract, good inspectors book fast
